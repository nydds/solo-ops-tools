What the two columns really are

An S corp is a tax election, not an entity

You do not form an S corporation at a state office. You form an LLC (or a corporation) and file Form 2553 with the IRS to be taxed under subchapter S. The company then files Form 1120-S each year and issues you a Schedule K-1 instead of the Schedule C you file now.

As a default sole proprietor or single-member LLC, all of your net profit is self-employment income: 15.3% on 92.35% of it — Social Security 12.4% up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500, Medicare 2.9% with no cap.

What the election changes

Under an S corp, your salary is a W-2 wage carrying the same 15.3% (half withheld from you, half paid by the company, both ultimately out of your business). The profit left after salary and costs is a distribution, and distributions carry no Social Security or Medicare tax at all.

That is the entire saving: 15.3% on the difference between profit and salary. Everything else about the election — extra filings, payroll, the reasonable-compensation requirement, state minimum taxes — is a cost against it.

How the estimate is calculated

  1. Sole prop / LLC. SE tax = 15.3% of 92.35% of profit (Social Security stops at $184,500; the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax starts at $200,000 single / $250,000 joint). Half of the SE tax is deductible, then the 2026 standard deduction, then the §199A deduction of 20% of qualified business income (profit minus half the SE tax). Federal income tax comes from the 2026 brackets.
  2. S corporation. Salary is capped at your profit. Payroll tax is 12.4% of salary up to the wage base plus 2.9% of salary. The company deducts the employer half of that, $42 of FUTA, your added costs, and any state S-corp tax. What is left is the K-1 distribution.
  3. Income tax on the S-corp side is computed on salary plus distribution. Wages are not qualified business income (the IRS says reasonable compensation from an S corp is not QBI), so the §199A deduction applies only to the distribution — which is why the income tax usually rises and eats part of the payroll-tax saving.
  4. Net saving = sole prop total cost minus S-corp total cost, where each total includes payroll or SE tax, federal income tax, and the cash costs of the election.
  5. Break-even profit scans net profit upward in $250 steps with your salary and your costs held fixed, and reports the first profit where the S corp is ahead.

The 1099 Tax Estimator on this site leaves the §199A deduction out, so its total can read higher than the sole-prop column here for the same profit.

Why the break-even is higher than the number you have read

The rule of thumb "an S corp pays off around $70,000–$90,000 of profit" comes from comparing payroll tax alone: 15.3% of profit versus 15.3% of salary, minus a $1,500–$2,500 bill. That comparison overstates the saving, because the election also costs you the deduction for half of SE tax and part of the §199A deduction — and part of the employer payroll tax is only deductible, not free.

Modeled in full, a $50,000 salary with $2,000 of running costs breaks even near $74,000 of profit; a $60,000 salary near $93,000; an $80,000 salary near $122,000. A higher, more defensible salary pushes break-even up — the same $60,000 salary with $4,000 of costs needs about $106,000. Above break-even the saving grows steadily: every $10,000 of profit more than your salary takes about $1,410 of self-employment tax off the table, of which roughly $1,250 survives the change in income tax.

Two things move it the other way. In a state like California that taxes the S corp at 1.5% of net income, and in any year you also have W-2 wages that already used up the Social Security wage base, the election is worth less.

What this leaves out

In the model's blind spot

  • State income tax on wages and distributions — the split mostly does not change it, but entity-level state taxes and city taxes do, and only a few are here.
  • The §199A wage limitation and the SSTB phase-out above the 2026 threshold ($201,750 single / $403,500 joint). The page warns you when you are in that range.
  • Health insurance, retirement contributions, an accountable plan, and QBI from other businesses.
  • The cost of the election itself: Form 2553, a new EIN, state registrations, and the first year of payroll setup.

What a CPA will also weigh

  • Whether your salary would survive an IRS reasonable-compensation challenge — the most common way an S corp saving turns into a bill.
  • Payroll deadlines, quarterly filings, and the fact that you cannot simply skip paying yourself in a bad month.
  • Whether you can reverse the election later: revocation needs IRS consent within five years in most cases.
  • Your total household picture, including a spouse's W-2 wages and the Additional Medicare Tax.

Questions this calculator gets

Is an S corp cheaper than an LLC?

It depends on the gap between your profit and your salary. The LLC has no payroll or 1120-S cost; the S corp saves 15.3% on everything above the salary. Below roughly $70,000–$100,000 of profit, depending on salary and costs, the election usually costs more than it saves. For formation and annual filing costs by state, see the LLC Cost Compare.

How low can my salary be?

There is no safe percentage in the law. It has to be what the business would pay someone else for the same work, considering your role, hours and experience. A salary that is 20% of profit is the pattern the IRS most often reclassifies — the page warns below 40%.

Does the calculator include state income tax?

No. Only federal tax plus the state S-corp-specific costs listed above. Add your state's income tax separately if you want a total picture; the salary/distribution split usually does not change state individual income tax much.

When should I make the election?

Form 2553 is generally due by March 15 of the year you want it to take effect (two months and 15 days into the tax year), or within 2 months and 15 days of forming the entity. Relief for a late election is available but not guaranteed — plan it with a CPA before the deadline passes.
